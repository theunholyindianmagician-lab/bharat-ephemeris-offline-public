वेध · the observation companion

The text predicts; if you wish, you observe with your own śaṅku and kapāla. The ledger keeps both, hash-chained, with each prediction written before its night. Every computation runs here, on your device; the line below says where the ledger is kept. If you keep a ledger, export it after every night and keep the file.

Observation is optional. Every computation is complete without it: every prediction on this page is computed by the choice below alone, and nothing recorded here changes a value the engine uses, on this page or on any other. What you record is kept beside the text, as an antara or a preview, never in its place.

The Sun, the stars, the noon shadow and the instruments are the Sūrya-Siddhānta's own model with Mādhava's sine (sphuta.js, ss-*.js, vedha-lekha.js) in every choice. The Moon's predictions — the crescent, the Moon on a junction star and the eclipses — follow the choice made here. Times are counts of your bowl from sunrise, in ghaṭī–vināḍī–prāṇa of the star-wheel's turn (SS 1.11-1.12). The rough hour beside each count only tells you when to be ready.

चन्द्र-गणना · the Moon's predictions are computed by

१ · स्थान — your place

Your latitude as you know it is enough; every prediction is computed for it without any observation. Give the palabhā (the equinox noon shadow) instead if you have it. The deśāntara is in degrees east (+) or west (−) of the Laṅkā–Ujjayinī meridian. If you observe, the Dhruva star's upper and lower meridian altitudes and the two solstice noons each give a latitude, and a timed lunar eclipse a deśāntara: the reduction sets each beside the place you gave, and never puts it in its place. A ledger keeps its place, so a new place means a new ledger.

२ · यन्त्र — the two instruments, as the text gives them

śilātale ambusaṃśuddhe vajralepe api vā same । tatra śaṅkvaṅgulairiṣṭaiḥ samaṃ maṇḍalaṃ ālikhet ।।
SS 3.1: on a level stone cleaned with water, or a hard plastered floor, draw a circle with the gnomon's aṅgulas as its measure.
tanmadhye sthāpayecchaṅkuṃ kalpanādvādaśāṅgulam । tacchāyāgraṃ spṛśedyatra vṛtte pūrvāparārdhayoḥ ।।
SS 3.2-3.3: set a gnomon of twelve aṅgulas at the centre. Where the shadow's tip touches the circle in the forenoon and the afternoon are west and east. Through their middle, by the "fish" (intersecting arcs), draw the north–south line.
tāmrapātraṃ adhaśchidraṃ nyastaṃ kuṇḍe amalāmbhasi । ṣaṣṭirmajjatyahorātre sphuṭaṃ yantraṃ kapālakam ।।
SS 13.23: a copper vessel with a hole beneath, set in a basin of clean water, sinks sixty times in a day and night: the kapāla.

SS 1.11-1.12: 6 prāṇa = 1 vināḍī, 60 vināḍī = 1 nāḍī (ghaṭī), and 60 nāḍī = one turn of the stars (the nākṣatra day). Your bowl is the clock; one star's two returns to the meridian are its calibration.

The first season (design §7.6)

If you choose to observe — nothing here is needed for the text's predictions — a first season can go like this.

  1. Calibrate the bowl. Start it when a bright junction star crosses your north–south line (a plumb-line over it). Count its sinkings until the same star crosses again. Record it as kapāla. Sixty is a perfect bowl; anything else is its rate, which the ledger applies to every count.
  2. Every clear noon, the shadow. Mark where the śaṅku's shadow crosses the north–south line, and measure it in aṅgula and vyaṅgula. Record it as madhyāhna.
  3. Stars on the meridian. Start the bowl at sunrise. Note the count when each star in tonight's list crosses the plumb-line, and its altitude if you have an arc. Record it as yāmyottara.
  4. The Dhruva star, twice. Its altitude when highest and when lowest: the mean is your latitude (SS 12.72-12.73).
  5. The crescent. On the evenings after amāvāsyā, look for it after sunset, and record both the evenings it is not seen and the evening it is. Record it as candra-darśana.
  6. Every visible lunar eclipse. Time sparśa, the middle and mokṣa by the bowl from that day's sunrise. Record it as grahaṇa.
  7. Your north–south line, checked. A star close to the Dhruva swings east and west of it. At its greatest swing east, and again at its greatest swing west (one long night, or two), read the arc from your line to the star and the side of the line it lies on. True north is the midpoint of the two (dhruva.js). Record both as one uttara-rekhā.
  8. The two solstice noons. On the noon nearest each solstice, the Sun's zenith distance (natāṃśa) and whether it stood south or north of the zenith. Half their difference is ε, the greatest declination; half their sum is your latitude. Record both as one ayanānta-yugma.

The last two are reduced geometrically and shown as a preview beside the text's own values — ε = arc of 1397/3438 (SS 2.28), which stays the default — and beside the place you gave. Nothing measured is applied or replaces a constant; using a measured value would be your own explicit choice. Refraction does not cancel in these readings, and nothing here removes it.

३ · गणित — the predictions for a day

Astronomical years: 0 = 1 BCE. The text's computations take any day from −50,000 to +50,000; the dṛk choice serves 1850–2150 only and says so outside it.

Your instruments (yantra.js)

Tick the instruments you have. Each line is the text's reading in the instrument's own graduation — a nata in ghaṭī–vināḍī–prāṇa from the meridian, an arc in aṃśa–kalā–vikalā — so no size is needed. The meridian wall and the ṣaṣṭhāṃśa read at the text's noon, the rāśivalaya at a sign's culmination, the rest at the bowl count from sunrise given here. The attribution of these instruments to Jai Singh II is unverified here; their geometry is derived from the sphere.
